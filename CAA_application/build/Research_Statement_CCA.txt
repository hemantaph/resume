Research statement
Phurailatpam Hemantakumar        |   Flatiron Research Fellow application

My thesis comprises work on gravitational-wave lensing statistics, SNR and detection-probability calculations,
and eccentric waveform modelling, implementation, and data analysis. I also pursue subsidiary projects on
line-of-sight acceleration and multimessenger lensing.

1. Gravitational-wave lensing statistics

Motivation and completed work. Strong lensing can magnify distant compact-binary mergers and produce
repeated observations of a source. Predicting the detectable population requires consistent source and lens
models, strong-lensing selection, lens-equation solutions, and detector selection. These predictions support
lensing searches and astrophysical and cosmological inference.
I lead the development of ler, an open-source Monte Carlo framework for lensed and unlensed GW
populations and detectable event rates [1]. I have implemented numerical strong-lensing cross-sections
for elliptical power-law lenses with external shear (EPL+Shear), together with optical-depth calculations.
Numba-compiled kernels, multithreading, and multiprocessing accelerate rejection and importance sampling,
inverse-transform sampling, parameter sampling, lens-equation solving, and numerical integration.
ler has been used in O3 lensing follow-up, faint-counterpart searches, and posterior-odds calculations [2, 3, 4].
I have implemented propagation of population-hyperparameter and finite-sampling uncertainties into event
rates. Further analysis and publication are ongoing.
Proposed research. My priority is to propagate uncertainties consistently from source and lens models
through to rates and detectable populations. I have begun work on star-formation-informed binary-black-hole
merger densities. I would convolve cosmic star formation with formation-to-merger delay distributions,
weighted by metallicity-dependent formation efficiency, and constrain the models using LVK data with detector
selection included. Nearby observations do not uniquely determine the distant population. Comparing
formation histories would quantify this uncertainty.
I would extend the uncertainty calculation to distributions of source parameters, magnifications, time delays,
and detectable image multiplicities. This requires propagating model alternatives, correlated hyperparameter
uncertainties, and finite Monte Carlo sampling errors through the same source, lens, and selection calculation.
Finite observed-catalogue statistics would enter the inference separately from numerical sampling error.
Source and lens models would be updated as constraints improve, and detector-selection uncertainties included.
The resulting distributions would supply population terms for Bayesian lensing tests.
Possible extension to cosmology. Maity et al. [5] study cosmological inference from the lensed-event
fraction and the distribution of lensing time delays, including detector selection. I would use ler to stress-test
this population-based time-delay cosmography with updated source and lens populations and EPL+Shear
lensing calculations. Model and parameter uncertainties would be propagated into the predicted observables
and cosmological inference, alongside finite-catalogue statistics and Monte Carlo error. Tests on simulated
catalogues would assess parameter bias, credible-interval coverage, and degeneracies between cosmology and
population assumptions. This would establish when the cosmological constraints remain reliable.
CCA connections. I am interested in collaboration with Will Farr and Jack Heinzel on population inference
and selection effects, and discussions across CCA’s GW, galaxy-formation, and cosmology groups on the
population models and cosmography tests. I would continue the associated IGWN work through GRASP,
including ISR-weighted ler development, testing, and support.

2. SNR and detection-probability calculations

Repeated detectability calculations are a computational bottleneck in GW population studies. I developed
gwsnr to calculate SNRs and detection probabilities efficiently [6]. It provides multiprocessing-based
waveform inner products, partial-scaling interpolation, and artificial-neural-network methods, with Numba,
JAX, and MLX supporting accelerated computation. The package has public documentation and is used in
collaborative research, including the detector-selection calculation in ler.
I plan to continue testing and improving these methods, including direct recalculation near the detection
boundary and comparison of SNR-based detection probabilities with injection recovery. This is needed to
quantify their accuracy and relation to search sensitivity. CCA’s expertise in selection effects and scientific
computing would support this work. I would continue development, testing, and user support as non-ISR
GRASP activity.

3. Eccentric waveform modelling, implementation, and data analysis

Motivation. Residual eccentricity can constrain compact-binary formation and evolution. Extracting it
requires accurate waveforms and tests of parameter degeneracies. This thesis topic combines analytical
modelling, waveform implementation, and Bayesian analysis of individual GW events.
Completed work. With collaborators, I developed TaylorF2Ecck and implemented it in LALSuite [7].
This frequency-domain inspiral model describes nonspinning, low-eccentricity binaries, with 𝑒 0 < 0.1
defined at 20 Hz. It consistently includes leading eccentricity contributions from orbital dynamics, periastron
advance, and GW emission, with phasing through 3PN order and quadrupolar-order amplitude. Analytical
and numerical checks preceded parameter-estimation and injection studies.
Analyses of GW170817 and GW190425 found no strong evidence favouring eccentricity or measurable
periastron-advance effects. Injection studies established where the waveform differences become distinguish-
able. We also found that insufficient phasing accuracy can bias the inferred mass ratio, motivating eccentric
phasing through at least 3.5PN order.
Proposed research and scientific benefit. I plan to extend TaylorF2Ecck with higher eccentricity orders,
phasing through at least 3.5PN order, amplitude corrections, and spin effects. Each extension requires
analytical consistency checks, implementation tests, comparisons with independent models, and injection
studies before application to observed events.
The aim is to determine which eccentricity effects are measurable and which can be confused with waveform
error. Follow-up studies would connect the resulting constraints to dynamical formation and other compact-
binary formation scenarios. Eccentricity alone does not uniquely identify a formation channel, so this
interpretation must retain model uncertainties and the relevant selection effects. At CCA, I am particularly
interested in collaboration with Max Isi on waveform systematics, model comparison, and inference
from individual GW events. I would contribute experience in post-Newtonian calculations, numerical
implementation, and Bayesian validation.

Subsidiary projects and prospects for extension

Line-of-sight acceleration

In a subsidiary project with Lalit Pathak and Achamveedu Gopakumar, we investigated eccentricity and
line-of-sight acceleration in GW190814 [8]. We used 32 seconds of data and the IMRPhenomXPHM waveform
augmented with leading-order acceleration and eccentricity corrections. The effects are strongly degenerate
in the inspiral phase. We found no significant Bayesian evidence for either effect relative to the baseline
model, despite nonzero parameter estimates in the joint analysis.
I would also revisit joint eccentricity and acceleration inference using improved waveform prescriptions,
including a more general treatment of time-dependent source acceleration. The motivation is to assess
whether environmental effects can be distinguished from residual eccentricity and waveform error, and what
they could reveal about a massive companion.

Multimessenger lensing

This subsidiary project aims to identify strongly lensed gamma-ray bursts (GRBs), with or without detectable
GW counterparts. With Anupreeta More, I forecast the joint detectability of lensed short GRBs and
binary-neutron-star GW signals using ler and gwsnr [9]. We considered GW counterparts to lensed GRBs
and GRB counterparts to lensed GW systems.
I am developing Bayesian posterior-odds tests of whether multiple GRBs are consistent with lensed images
of one source, initially using sky localisation and time delays. Possible extensions would incorporate
light-curve information and GW–GRB associations. The tests must account for measurement uncertainties,
unrelated-burst coincidences, lensing-rate priors, and GRB selection. Validation with simulated lensed and
unrelated events would establish how performance depends on these assumptions.
The immediate goal is a statistical framework and code for candidate assessment. In the longer term, joint
lensed observations could constrain source and lens properties and, with suitable electromagnetic observations
and lens modelling, support time-delay cosmography. CCA’s expertise in Bayesian inference and GW
cosmology would be valuable for developing these extensions. This work would complement my main thesis
directions.

Broader contribution

ler is a community-driven project with well-written documentation and actively maintained, regularly
updated code. Contributions and feedback from users inform its development.
I would bring experience developing documented scientific software, checking analytical models, and carrying
numerical calculations through to data analysis. CCA’s statistical expertise and scientific-computing support
would help me improve both the efficiency and validation of these projects. I would share reproducible
examples, simulation products, and research code, while continuing user support for my packages.
I have mentored undergraduate, M.Sc., and early-stage PhD researchers, taught scientific Python at CUHK,
and conducted GW data-analysis workshops in Manipur. I would contribute to CCA’s mentoring and
computational training through similar practical work. These activities make the methods accessible to
researchers who want to test their own astrophysical assumptions and reproduce the analysis.
